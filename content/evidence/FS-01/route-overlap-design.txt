Berea Rides matches riders to drivers who are already going their way. The
interesting part is not the matching itself, which is a distance check against a
route polyline. It is that the gate exists for a legal reason, that the first two
versions of it were wrong in a way that only showed up with real users, and that
the fix was to make the algorithm less clever rather than more.

WHY THERE IS A GATE AT ALL

Kentucky regulates for-hire transport. KRS 281.605(10) carves out an exemption
for carpools, but only where the trip is incidental to the driver's own commute.
A driver going to their campus worksite who takes a passenger along the way is
carpooling. The same driver making a detour to collect a passenger is running a
for-hire trip, and the exemption no longer applies.

That distinction is the product. If the app cannot tell the two apart, it is not
a carpool app. So the matching rule is not a relevance heuristic that makes the
feed nicer — it is the mechanism that keeps the service inside the exemption, and
it has to hold at the point a booking is created rather than as a suggestion.

VERSION ONE: BOTH ENDPOINTS IN A CORRIDOR

The first rule was the cheapest thing that could work. Fetch the driver's route
from the Directions API, decode the polyline, and require that the rider's pickup
and dropoff each fall within 500 metres of some sample on it. 500 metres is about
a third of a mile — tight enough that a detour across town fails, loose enough to
absorb GPS drift and the fact that an overview polyline is sampled, not
continuous.

It needed no route lookup for the rider, which mattered because every Directions
call costs money and latency. A driver whose own directions fetch failed matched
nobody, which is the correct failure direction: no match is a worse experience
than a bad match, but a bad match is a legal problem.

VERSION TWO: PERCENTAGE OF THE RIDER'S ROUTE

Two endpoints inside a corridor does not mean the journey between them follows
it. A rider could plausibly start and end near the driver's route while the path
between them diverges. So the second version fetched the rider's polyline too and
measured how much of it lay inside the driver's corridor: for each segment of the
rider's route, test whether the midpoint is within 500 metres of the driver's
polyline, and report the fraction that are. A match required at least 80 percent.

Segment midpoints are a proxy for "most of this segment is inside the corridor",
which is not exact but is close enough at the sampling density the API returns.
The cost is the rider's polyline points multiplied by the driver's — a few
hundred by a few hundred, trivial on a phone.

WHAT BROKE

The 80 percent gate rejected trips that were obviously carpools.

A driver commuting from Berea to Richmond would match a rider whose pickup was a
campus residence hall, because that sits on the route. The same driver would be
blocked from a rider at the Berea Walmart, 1.5 kilometres off the polyline —
a deviation of a couple of minutes, in the driver's own town, in the direction
they were already travelling. Nobody would describe that as a for-hire trip. The
algorithm did, because 1.5 kilometres is more than 500 metres and the rule could
not see anything else.

The gate was measuring geometry when the statute asks about intent. Tightening it
further, or tuning the corridor width, would have moved the false-rejection line
around without addressing that the algorithm was answering the wrong question.

VERSION THREE: A LOOSER GATE, AND A HUMAN

The current rule keeps only the part an algorithm is actually good at. A rider's
pickup must be within 10 kilometres of the driver's pickup, and their dropoff
within 10 kilometres of the driver's dropoff. 10 kilometres is wider than any
town in range — Berea is about 5 kilometres across, Richmond about 8 — so anyone
in the same town matches, and a Berea-to-Cincinnati rider still fails against a
Berea-to-Richmond driver.

The commute test moved to the driver. At the accept or decline step the driver
sees the rider's exact pickup and dropoff and decides whether it is on their way.
That is the judgement the statute actually describes, and the person making it is
the one whose commute it is.

This is a real trade. An algorithmic threshold is auditable: it produces the same
answer every time and can be shown to a regulator. Driver discretion cannot be
audited the same way, and it puts a compliance decision in the hands of someone
who is not thinking about statutes. The mitigation is that the coarse gate still
blocks the cross-region cases that would be indefensible, and the driver only
ever chooses within a set that has already passed it.

TWO THINGS THAT FELL OUT OF THE CHANGE

The client and the database enforce the same rule with the same numbers. The
booking path runs the check on the device, and a Postgres trigger runs it again
on insert. If those two disagreed, a request the app accepted could be rejected
by the database, and the user would see a failure with no explanation. Changing
the threshold means changing both, together.

The stored pass flag is frozen at insert. Loosening the gate did not retroactively
pass the requests that the strict rule had already rejected, so pending requests
needed a backfill migration. A rule that writes its verdict into a row rather than
recomputing it is faster to query and harder to change, and that cost only becomes
visible the first time the rule changes.

WHAT IS NOT PROVEN HERE

The current gate has not been measured against real traffic. There is no
false-rejection rate, no record of how often drivers decline a request the
algorithm allowed, and no comparison against a naive proximity baseline. The
argument above is a design rationale, not an evaluation, and it should be read as
one until those numbers exist.
